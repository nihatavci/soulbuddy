# "Remove Ghosting Forever" — Analysis of a 24h Post-Match Exclusivity Lock

**Date:** 2026-08-31
**Scope:** Product-decision research on the proposal to lock both parties out of opening any new mutual resonance/private space for 24 hours after a match, marketed as "we removed ghosting forever."
**Method:** Primary-source repo inspection (code + PRD) + external web research on real dating-app precedent and press reaction. Every external claim below is cited; where I could not find primary-source confirmation, that is stated explicitly rather than filled in.

---

## 0. What the repo actually shows (grounding facts)

- **The match event (`open_space()`)** — `supabase/migrations/009_resense_signals_loop.sql` lines 109–127: a trigger on `replies` insert, `security definer`, unconditionally does `insert into private_spaces (...) on conflict (signal_id, user_b) do nothing`. There is **no existing gating, cooldown, or eligibility check** anywhere in this function or the `private_spaces` table (no insert/update/delete RLS policy at all — "rows are created ONLY by open_space()"). A 24h lock would have to be added here, almost certainly as a new precondition query against the user's other `private_spaces.created_at` rows (or a new per-user "locked_until" column) before the insert is allowed to proceed — today nothing stops two simultaneous matches for the same user.
- **The only existing cap in the product** — `constants/signals.ts`: `DAILY_SIGNAL_CAP = 3`, explicitly commented `// not enforced server-side yet (deferred)`. This is a *posting* cap (how many signals a user can drop per day), not a matching/connection cap, and it is *not even enforced yet*. There is no precedent in this codebase for enforcing a hard *relationship-state* lock (as opposed to a content-posting throttle) anywhere in the system.
- **The silent-space ritual** — `hooks/useSpaceClosurePrompt.ts`: triggers only after **14 days** (`SPACE_CLOSURE_SILENCE_DAYS`) of silence in a space, is dismissible, local-only (MMKV, never synced), and explicitly designed to *not* judge or force anything — "not a wrong answer," "never navigates or blocks." It is a *very* slow, gentle, opt-in acknowledgment mechanic, philosophically the opposite of a hard 24h lock.
- **Safety flows** — `app/(app)/check-in.tsx` and `app/(app)/private-space.tsx`: Report and Block live in the *same* single-select list as the self-check-in "how does this feel" options, always available as "a persistent strip" (Reveal / Slow down / Report) one tap away inside the space itself. Blocking is described in-app as "Block & end conversation with {alias}" — i.e., blocking is explicitly the *exit* mechanism from a space.
- **PRD non-goals** (`PRD/PRD.md`): "No streaks. No read receipts. No urgency monetization. No public follower graph." PRD explicitly tracks **"Ghosting rate after resonance unlock"** as a success metric (line 144) and "**>25 reports / 1,000 private space entries**" as a kill metric (line 147) — so the product already treats ghosting as a *measured outcome*, not something to be claimed "solved" outright, and treats *report volume* as a safety tripwire.

These facts matter directly to findings 3–5 below.

## 1. Mechanism precision: does a 24h new-match lock change whether ghosting happens in the EXISTING match?

**Definition, from primary sources.** Ghosting is consistently defined across academic and clinical sources as the abrupt, unexplained cessation of communication with someone with whom contact was already established — not a failure to *initiate* contact. Ghosting research and complaint patterns (see [Timmermans, Hermans & Opree, 2021, *Gone with the wind: Exploring mobile daters' ghosting experiences*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407520970287); [Psychology Today, "Ghosting"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/ghosting); [The Conversation, "Ghosting and 'breadcrumbing'"](https://theconversation.com/ghosting-and-breadcrumbing-the-psychological-impact-of-our-bad-behaviour-on-dating-apps-258087); [Forbes Health/OnePoll survey, "Survey Reveals 'Ghosting' Impacts 76% Of People Who Are Dating"](https://www.forbes.com/health/mind/modern-dating-mental-health/)) converge on ghosting as a *unilateral silent exit from an existing connection*, most often attributed by ghosters themselves to low investment, conflict avoidance, or simply having a better option elsewhere (attention diverted to a new match is *one* driver among several — boredom, incompatibility discovered mid-conversation, or plain avoidance are others).

**The mechanism under review does not touch that exit path at all.** A 24h lock on opening a *new* private space:
- Does **not** require either person to send a message, respond, or otherwise act inside the *existing* space.
- Does **not** prevent either person from simply not opening the app, not typing, or going silent in the private space they are already in.
- Does **not** create any consequence, prompt, or friction tied to silence in the current space — that remains governed entirely by `useSpaceClosurePrompt`'s 14-day, dismissible, non-blocking ritual.
- Only removes **one possible cause** of one flavor of ghosting: "I stopped replying to you because someone else showed up." It does nothing about the far more commonly cited causes (loss of interest, discomfort, conflict avoidance, forgetting, or simply not wanting to have the "let's stop talking" conversation) — see the ghosting-motivation literature above, where "just got busy and forgot" and "lost interest" dominate stated reasons over "found someone else," which is not even a top-cited cause in these surveys.

**Verdict on Finding 1:** This is a **precise category error** if marketed as "removed ghosting." The mechanism at best slightly reduces the *incidence of one specific driver* of premature disengagement — attention-splitting across simultaneous connections — for exactly the 24-hour window in which a genuinely new match happening simultaneously would have been the cause. It does not prevent, detect, discourage, or provide any recourse for ghosting itself (silent exit from the existing thread), which can and will still happen for the other 90%+ of stated reasons, and can still happen *after* the 24 hours lapse regardless. "Solves attention-splitting for 24h" and "removed ghosting forever" are not the same claim, and the gap between them is large enough to be a false claim on its face, not a rounding error.

## 2. Real precedent search: match-exclusivity / match-limiting / cooldown-after-match mechanics

### Hinge — "Your Turn" (2017), the closest real precedent to an explicit "reduce ghosting" feature
TechCrunch's contemporaneous coverage ([TechCrunch, Dec 20 2017, "Dating app Hinge rolls out a new feature to reduce 'ghosting'"](https://techcrunch.com/2017/12/20/dating-app-hinge-rolls-out-a-new-feature-to-reduce-ghosting/)) describes the actual mechanic: a feature that flagged unanswered messages, nudged whoever owed a response, and let users hide (not just unmatch) stale matches. Hinge attributed the behavior partly to forgetfulness, citing internal data that "23 percent of the time, they just got busy and forgot," and claimed a 25% reduction in matches-that-don't-lead-to-a-conversation from limited market tests (London, DC). This is the **structurally correct kind of anti-ghosting intervention** — it targets the actual mechanism (forgetting/avoidance to respond) with a reminder/nudge inside the *existing* conversation, not a lockout on new connections. I could not find independent press or user follow-up coverage of "Your Turn"'s longer-term reception or retention — Hinge's product surface today (nudges, "Your Turn" labeling in the inbox) suggests the underlying nudge concept persisted in some form, but I am not asserting that with a citation I can stand behind; flagging this gap explicitly rather than guessing.

### Hinge — "Standouts" (current)
[Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/hinge-reveals-standouts-and-roses/) and secondary sources describe Standouts as a curated daily feed of high-compatibility profiles, gated by a "Rose" (a stronger like) to engage from it. This is a **discovery-curation** feature, not an exclusivity lock — it doesn't restrict a user from having multiple simultaneous matches/conversations. Not a real precedent for the mechanic under review; included because the brief named it, and it's worth being precise that it doesn't actually match the shape of "lock out new matches."

### Coffee Meets Bagel — daily-limited-matches ("Bagel") model
Multiple review sources (e.g. [SwipeStats](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/coffee-meets-bagel-review), [DatingAdvice](https://www.datingadvice.com/online-dating/coffee-meets-bagel-review-how-it-works)) describe CMB as rationing the *supply* of daily suggested matches (curated "bagels" delivered once daily, capped likes), plus a **7-day chat expiry** after a match to force a decision. This limits volume and adds time-pressure to *convert* a match into contact info/a date — it is not a lock preventing new matches while an existing chat is open. Notably, CMB's own model still lets a user run several conversations in parallel; the constraint is on *how many new matches arrive per day*, not on *whether you can pursue more than one thread at once*. This is meaningfully different from the proposed mechanic and is not real precedent for exclusivity-lock, though it is precedent for "the product intentionally rations volume as anti-overwhelm design," which the PRD's existing `DAILY_SIGNAL_CAP` already mirrors.

### "One match at a time" apps — Cuffed, OnlyOne, OneMatch, Delight
These are the closest real precedent for the *literal mechanic* — restrict a user to a single active match/conversation. Findings on **Cuffed** specifically (the most discussed of these):
- [Hacker News discussion, Feb 2022](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=30393899) surfaced two structural objections from commenters building/evaluating similar products: (a) **marketplace-imbalance risk** — "If you have more men than women, you either need to give some men no matches on some days...or give women multiple potential matches" (user *dlevine*, referencing Coffee Meets Bagel hitting this exact problem) — i.e., a hard one-at-a-time cap breaks down under real supply/demand skew; (b) a **monetization-conflict** objection — "Dating apps should not be profitable. It is an extreme conflict of interest against successfully matching users" (user *Wiseacre*) — relevant to any product (re:sense included) layering monetization onto a scarcity mechanic later.
- Later app-store/review aggregation ([opentools.ai summary](https://opentools.ai/tools/cuffed-dating)) reports users specifically **praising a subsequent "3 matches at a time" update** as more efficient than the original strict one-at-a-time constraint, alongside complaints about paywalls unrelated to the exclusivity mechanic itself. Read plainly: **Cuffed shipped strict one-match exclusivity and then loosened it** to multiple simultaneous matches, because users found the original constraint too restrictive — a real, if modest, revert-under-user-pressure signal for the *permanent, indefinite* version of this mechanic (re:sense's proposal is temporary — 24h — which is a materially softer version, discussed in Finding 5).

### Tinder / Bumble / Hinge "pause new matches" style features
I searched specifically for match-limiting or cooldown-after-match features on the three majors and did **not** find any that restrict a user to one active connection or lock out new matches after a match occurs. What exists instead are user-initiated, symmetric-effort-neutral controls: Bumble's Snooze ("go invisible," matches without active chats expire — [Bumble Support](https://support.bumble.com/hc/en-us/articles/28537106111261-Taking-a-break-from-online-dating)) and Hinge's free profile-pause (hides you from new people). Critically, **these are opt-in, user-controlled pauses that a person chooses for themselves** — not a system-imposed lock triggered automatically by an external event (a match happening) and applied without the user's choice. I found no major dating app that has shipped a mandatory, system-triggered exclusivity lockout of the kind under review. **This means the proposed mechanic has essentially no precedent among high-scale, general-purpose dating apps** — the only real analogs are niche "intentional dating" apps (Cuffed etc.) explicitly built around permanent one-at-a-time scarcity as their entire premise, not as a bolt-on anti-ghosting feature layered onto an otherwise normal matching flow.

**Implication for re:sense:** re:sense is not built, from the ground up, as a one-match-at-a-time product (its core loop is Signal → Reply → Resonance → Space, open-ended on how many spaces can exist). Grafting a temporary exclusivity lock onto that architecture as a headline anti-ghosting feature is closer to CMB/Cuffed's scarcity experiments than to anything the majors have shipped, and the one closest real-world analog to the *permanent* version of this idea (Cuffed) was **loosened after shipping**, not tightened.

## 3. Second-order / failure-mode analysis specific to re:sense's actual architecture

### 3.1 Interaction with `DAILY_SIGNAL_CAP`
`DAILY_SIGNAL_CAP = 3` is a posting cap, not a matching cap, and is explicitly "not enforced server-side yet (deferred)." A 24h match-lock is a different axis (connections open, not signals posted) and would compound with it in one specific way: a user who is locked out of new matches can *still* post up to 3 signals/day and *still* receive replies — those replies would need new handling, because `open_space()` currently opens a space unconditionally on first reply. If the lock is enforced by blocking `open_space()` from firing, the *other* person's reply — a genuine, good-faith act of reaching out — would silently fail to produce a space for 24h. That is a real product-quality regression: someone who put effort into replying gets no resonance because of a rule that has nothing to do with them. The PRD's own metric "reply-per-view" would be undermined by an invisible mechanic that makes some fraction of genuine replies not resolve into a space, with no explanation surfaced to either party. This needs an explicit UX answer (queue the space to auto-open at lock expiry? reject the reply outright with a message? show the replier why nothing happened?) that does not currently exist anywhere in the schema or app.

### 3.2 Interaction with `useSpaceClosurePrompt` (does the lock make silence MORE likely?)
This is a legitimate and somewhat counter-intuitive risk. The closure-ritual's entire premise is that a space quiets *naturally* over 14 days and the product should acknowledge that gently rather than force anything. A 24h exclusivity lock changes the *incentive structure* right after a match: someone who, within the first day, senses the new connection isn't working for them (personality mismatch, tone doesn't land, etc.) has **no low-friction, low-conflict alternative** — normally, on re:sense's current architecture, they could let one match go quiet while a second one develops in parallel, softening the emotional weight of any one conversation not working out. Locking out new matches removes that pressure-release valve for 24h. Two plausible outcomes, both bad in different ways: (a) it can *increase* the chance the person disengages harder and faster from the trapped connection (accelerating, not preventing, silence — the opposite of the stated goal), or (b) it pressures people to fake continued engagement in a connection they've already mentally exited, which is a worse outcome than honest silence because it's manufactured, low-quality interaction inside a product whose stated core value is "feeling heard," not "feeling obligated." Neither of these is something the existing 14-day, dismissible, non-judgmental closure ritual was designed to handle — the ritual assumes closure is unforced and natural; the lock actively removes the "just let this one go quietly and see who else shows up" option that makes closure unforced in the first place.

### 3.3 Interaction with safety flows (Report/Block as escape hatch)
This is the most serious concern, and it's grounded directly in what `check-in.tsx` and `private-space.tsx` already do. Report and Block currently live together as "Safety" actions, and Block is explicitly described as ending the conversation ("Block & end conversation with {alias}"). If a 24h lock is enforced at the account level (both people can't open ANY new space, regardless of which existing space they're in), then:

- **A user whose first match is a bad-faith actor, a harasser, or an immediate red flag has no legitimate way out except Block/Report for the remainder of the 24h window** — they cannot simply let the match go quiet and see who else replies to their signal, because the lock prevents any new space regardless of what happens in the bad one. This directly conflicts with the product's own safety positioning: a safety system that only offers "report/block this person" or "wait out the clock trapped with a red flag" as the two exits is coercive by construction, not a neutral design choice.
- **This creates a real incentive to misuse Block/Report as a pure "let me out of this lock" mechanism**, independent of whether anything unsafe actually happened — someone who's simply bored or unmatched in interest, and wants a second connection back, has a one-tap way to manufacture an exit: report or block, which (presumably) would need to release the lock to make any sense at all as a safety design. That inflates report/block volume with non-safety-motivated taps, which directly undermines the PRD's own **kill metric — ">25 reports / 1,000 private space entries"** (PRD.md line 147). A mechanic that structurally incentivizes gaming the report flow to escape an unrelated product lock is a bad interaction with a metric the team has already flagged as a kill-switch signal, and it would make that signal *noisier and less trustworthy* at exactly the moment it matters most (distinguishing real safety issues from lock-escape taps becomes a new triage burden with no existing tooling for it).
- Alternatively, if Block/Report does **not** release the lock (to avoid the gaming incentive above), the trapped-with-a-red-flag problem in the first bullet remains unsolved and arguably worsens — now there's no exit at all until the clock runs out, for a person who may have genuine safety reasons to want a way to a different, safer connection sooner. Either resolution of this fork produces a bad outcome; that's a signal the mechanism has a structural conflict with the product's safety architecture rather than an implementation detail to be tuned.

### 3.4 Locked decisions / non-goals check
PRD non-goals explicitly rule out "urgency monetization" and (per the mission brief) "no dark patterns," "no fake urgency." A mandatory, system-imposed 24h lockout — framed publicly as a virtue rather than disclosed as a constraint — sits close to that line: it manufactures a forced-scarcity condition on user behavior (you may not do X for N hours, regardless of what X's context is) which is a classic dark-pattern shape (imposed friction dressed as a feature) even though the *intent* here is prosocial rather than extractive. Intent doesn't change the mechanism's shape from the user's seat: being told "you can't talk to anyone else right now" is externally imposed urgency/restriction, which is exactly the category the product has committed to avoiding, even when the marketing frame is "this is for your own good."

## 4. Marketing-claim honesty check

**Bumble — "women message first."** Multiple 2026 press pieces cover Bumble abandoning its founding, decade-plus "women message first" rule. [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/esther-lian/bumble-dropping-the-rule-that-defined-it-for-twelve-years/91388972) (headline: "Bumble Is Dropping the Rule That Defined It for 12 Years. Now Experts Say It Could Lose Its Edge") and [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/GMA/Living/bumbles-women-messaging/story?id=135646428) both frame this as Bumble abandoning the mechanic that *was* its brand identity and core differentiator claim. [NOW Toronto](https://nowtoronto.com/lifestyle/bumble-women-message-first-rule-toronto-daters/) captured mixed-to-skeptical user reaction to the *reversal* itself ("A lot of creepy men out there," per one dater quoted), and coverage separately notes Bumble's own internal data cited as justification (nearly 66% of women reportedly preferring men message first) — implying the original absolutist framing ("women message first," full stop) had already been quietly under strain before the public reversal. I was **not able to fetch** the specific Jezebel piece skeptical of Bumble's motives (403 blocked) so I am not quoting it directly, but the pattern across the sources I *could* read is consistent: a bold, identity-defining claim, held for years, was reversed under real-world pressure once it stopped matching what a majority of the affected users said they actually wanted — and press covered the reversal as a story about the brand's core promise breaking, not as a neutral feature update.

**Hinge — "designed to be deleted."** This tagline has drawn sustained, specific skepticism for exactly the reason relevant here: a **structural conflict between the claim and the business model**. [Latana](https://resources.latana.com/post/hinge-deep-dive/) and a critical [Medium piece, "Hinge WAS NOT Designed to be Deleted"](https://medium.com/@thenathanchen/hinge-was-not-designed-to-be-deleted-d2ca9c8db64e) both make the same core argument: Hinge earns revenue from people continuing to swipe/pay for visibility features, which is in tension with a promise to get people to leave; a Substack piece titled ["3 Reasons Hinge ISN'T 'Designed to be Deleted'"](https://interfaceofus.substack.com/p/3-reasons-why-hinge-isnt-designed) makes the same "well actually" critique explicit in its title. The recurring press/user pattern across almost a decade of commentary on this tagline is: **an absolutist, definitive-sounding brand claim gets picked apart the moment someone checks it against the actual mechanism/incentives**, and once picked apart, it becomes a recurring point of mockery rather than fading quietly.

**Applying the same lens to "we removed ghosting forever."** Per Finding 1, this claim is *more* exposed than either precedent above, for two reasons: (a) both Bumble's and Hinge's claims were at least broadly *aligned* with what their mechanism does most of the time (Bumble genuinely did require women to message first for years; Hinge genuinely is used by many people who do delete it after finding a relationship) — the criticism was about edge cases and incentive misalignment, not about the claim being flatly false. "We removed ghosting forever" is a much stronger, more falsifiable claim than either of those, and per Finding 1 the mechanism doesn't prevent ghosting in the existing match at all — it only removes one contributing cause, for a 24-hour window, for new matches only. Any journalist, competitor, or even an ordinary user who gets ghosted by their match on day 2 (post-lock) or ghosted for any of the majority of non-"new-match-distraction" reasons on day 1 has a trivial, concrete counterexample. (b) I found **no existing precedent of a dating app making a comparably absolute anti-ghosting promise ("removed forever")** — Hinge's 2017 "Your Turn" was marketed as a feature that "reduces" ghosting with a measured 25% figure, not an eliminated-forever claim; that phrasing choice (measured reduction vs. absolute elimination) is itself instructive and is the more defensible frame if re:sense wants to make any public claim in this space at all.

## 5. Grounded verdict

**Recommendation: Drop the mechanism as specified (mandatory 24h system-imposed match-exclusivity lock) and drop the "removed ghosting forever" claim entirely, regardless of what happens to the mechanism.**

Reasoning, tied to re:sense's actual locked constraints and architecture rather than generic dating-app advice:

1. **The claim is not honest, and re:sense doesn't need a claim like this to fail publicly to know that** — Finding 1 shows the mechanism addresses one contributing driver of one flavor of premature disengagement (attention-splitting on brand-new matches), while ghosting (silent exit from an *existing* connection, per the literature) remains entirely possible before, during, and after the lock window. Finding 4 shows that even *more defensible*, hedged absolutist dating-app claims ("designed to be deleted," "women message first") have drawn years of "well actually" scrutiny; an outright-false "forever" claim is strictly worse exposure with no comparable upside, and directly contradicts the product's own "no dark patterns" positioning the moment anyone checks the mechanism against the marketing.
2. **The mechanism itself conflicts with existing, already-shipped safety architecture** (Finding 3.3) in a way that has no clean resolution: either it traps someone with a bad-faith match with no low-friction exit, or it turns Report/Block into a lock-escape hatch that pollutes the PRD's own kill-metric signal (>25 reports/1,000 space entries). This is not a hypothetical edge case — it's a direct, structural consequence of layering an unconditional lock on top of an unconditional `open_space()` trigger and a Report/Block flow that is the *only* other exit surfaced to users in `private-space.tsx`.
3. **It plausibly makes the exact problem it targets worse**, per Finding 3.2 — removing the "let one match go quiet while pursuing another" pressure-release option that the *existing* `useSpaceClosurePrompt` design implicitly assumes is available (14-day, dismissible, "never navigates or blocks" — designed around unforced natural closure, not forced continuation).
4. **There is essentially no real precedent for the general-purpose-dating-app version of this mechanic**, and the closest actual precedent (Cuffed's strict one-match-at-a-time) was *loosened*, not tightened, after real user feedback (Finding 2) — which is a weak-to-negative signal for shipping a stricter, system-imposed (not user-chosen) version of the same idea on a product that isn't built around single-match exclusivity as its core premise.
5. **It sits close to the PRD's own "no urgency monetization"/"no dark patterns" non-goals** even though its intent is prosocial — a system-imposed behavioral restriction, marketed as a virtue rather than disclosed as a constraint, is the same *shape* as the manipulative patterns the product has committed to avoiding, independent of intent (Finding 3.4).

**If the underlying goal — reduce ghosting after resonance — is still worth pursuing, the evidence above points toward a different, cheaper, and more honest intervention:** target the mechanism ghosting research actually identifies (forgetting, avoidance, low-effort silent exit) *inside the existing match*, the way Hinge's 2017 "Your Turn" did — e.g., a gentle nudge/reminder when a reply is owed, or leaning further into what `useSpaceClosurePrompt` already does well (calm acknowledgment of natural closure) rather than adding forced restriction. That approach doesn't require gating `open_space()`, doesn't interact badly with Report/Block, doesn't need a marketing claim strong enough to invite scrutiny, and is consistent with the product's already-established, evidently deliberate design philosophy of gentle, non-blocking, dismissible prompts rather than hard locks.

## Recommendations & Next Steps

**Do:**

1. **Do not build** the 24h mandatory match-exclusivity lock, in any form, mandatory or opt-in-but-defaulted-on. The safety-flow conflict in §3.3 (Report/Block either traps a bad-faith match or becomes a lock-escape hatch) is not fixable by tuning the lock duration — it's inherent to *any* mandatory lock layered on the current `open_space()` + Report/Block architecture. Shortening it to 6h or 12h doesn't remove the conflict, it just shrinks the window.
2. **Do not ship the phrase "we removed ghosting" (or any absolute variant — "ghosting-free," "no more ghosting")** anywhere: onboarding, App Store copy, marketing, PRD language. Per §4, this is a more falsifiable claim than either Bumble's or Hinge's headline promises, both of which drew years of scrutiny while being *broadly true*. This one is false on day one for the majority of ghosting's actual causes.
3. **Build a "your turn" nudge instead** — the closest real, working precedent (Hinge 2017, §2). Concretely, scoped to this codebase:
   - New hook, `hooks/useReplyOwedPrompt.ts`, mirroring `useSpaceClosurePrompt.ts`'s shape: pure, testable logic taking a space + its messages, returning whether the *current user* owes the next reply and how long it's been (e.g. `>48h` since the other person's last message with no reply back).
   - Surface it as a soft, dismissible nudge inside `private-space.tsx` — a `SystemNote`-style inline note ("it's your turn — {alias} is waiting to hear back"), not a push notification, not a badge, not anything read-receipt-adjacent. No visibility to the other person that a nudge fired.
   - No lock, no restriction on opening new spaces, no forced consequence — purely a memory-jog for the "23% just got busy and forgot" case Hinge's own data named (§2). This is a `Zeigarnik effect`-shaped nudge (an unfinished, owed action), not a scarcity/urgency mechanic, so it doesn't touch the "no fake urgency" non-goal.
   - If you want a metric to justify it later, track "replies sent within 24h of a your-turn nudge firing" as an internal signal — never surface a streak or count to the user.
4. **Keep `useSpaceClosurePrompt` exactly as it is** — §3.2 shows the *proposed* lock would have undermined it; the nudge in (3) is additive and doesn't touch its 14-day, dismissible, non-judgmental design.
5. **If a public claim is wanted eventually**, use Hinge's own framing discipline as the model: a hedged, measured claim tied to a real internal metric ("we send a gentle reminder when it's your turn to reply" / "X% fewer conversations go unanswered"), never an absolute "forever" claim — and only after you actually have the internal data to back the number, the way Hinge cited its 25% figure from real market tests before making any public claim.

**Open question for a product decision, not a research one:** whether a "your turn" nudge is worth building at all right now, given it's net-new surface area (a new hook, new UI, a threshold to tune) for a problem the PRD already tracks passively as a metric ("ghosting rate after resonance unlock") rather than actively intervenes on today. That's a scope/priority call, not something this research can settle — happy to draft it out further (data model, exact copy, threshold tuning) if you want to move on it.
